Theory: what the literature says

The theory behind my master's thesis: six chapters and a note on method. These are working drafts, and every fact links to where I found it.

★Where my research stands

My draft conclusion so far. Policy in Iceland and in Europe defines food sustainability broadly, but when it talks about education it means consumer information or training for professionals. Sustainability is a pillar of the Icelandic curriculum, applied unevenly, and I found almost nothing linking it to farms or to non-formal learning for people aged 16–30. None of the European programmes I could verify is aimed at that age group, mixes farm and digital learning, or is designed together with young people and farmers. Most of the evidence is self-reported or short-term. Small farms rarely have a plan for digital communication, and newcomers to farming are cut off from the knowledge networks. The gap I want to fill: farm-based and digital learning for young people in Iceland, designed with them and with farmers, that teaches and is also useful to the farms.

Every factual sentence below links to its source. My own readings are marked “Draft interpretation”. What I still need to check is listed at the end.

T1Sustainability, narrowed to food sustainability

I start from the definition most people still quote, from the 1996 World Food Summit: "Food security exists when all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient" safe and nutritious food that meets their dietary needs and preferences for an active and healthy life FAO 1996. That definition is about access. Food systems thinking asks more of it. FAO uses the High Level Panel of Experts' wording, where a sustainable food system "delivers food security and nutrition for all" without eroding the economic, social and environmental bases that future generations will need FAO 2018; FAO SFS Programme flyer. On diets, a symposium at FAO in Rome in November 2010 agreed that sustainable diets have low environmental impact and are "protective and respectful of biodiversity and ecosystems, culturally acceptable", as well as affordable, adequate, safe and healthy Burlingame & Dernini 2012. The EAT-Lancet Commission then attached figures. Feeding around 10 billion people by 2050, it argued, means changing diets, production and waste together: red meat and sugar cut by more than half, and nuts, fruit, vegetables and legumes more than doubled Willett et al. 2019; Stockholm Resilience Centre 2019.

All of this sits under the 2030 Agenda, adopted by the UN General Assembly on 25 September 2015 UN 2015. Two targets in SDG 12 matter here. Target 12.3 is to "halve per capita global food waste at the retail and consumer levels" by 2030, and target 12.8 is to make sure people everywhere have "the relevant information and awareness" for sustainable lifestyles UNRIC n.d.. For me, 12.8 is the line that ties food sustainability to education. In Iceland, the food policy to 2040 (Matvælastefna Íslands til ársins 2040, June 2023) names six focus areas: sustainable production, society, food security, food safety, consumer needs, and research, innovation and education. Its vision includes carbon-neutral food production Matvælaráðuneytið 2023. Alþingi adopted it as a parliamentary resolution (Þingsályktun um matvælastefnu til ársins 2040, þskj. 1913, 153rd session) Alþingi 2023. The first action plan for organic production came out as a draft in November 2023 Stjórnarráð Íslands 2023, and the final version in August 2024. It aims for "10% af landbúnaðarlandi á Íslandi verði komið með lífræna vottun 2040", with actions on consumer education and producer training Matvælaráðuneytið 2024. The EU's Farm to Fork strategy, by comparison, aims for at least 25% organic land by 2030 Stjórnarráð Íslands 2023.

Three labels get mixed up all the time, on farm signs and in conversation, so I keep them apart. "Organic" is a legal, certified category. In the EU it falls under Regulation (EU) 2018/848, which requires checks at every stage and makes the EU logo compulsory on prepacked organic food EU 2018. IFOAM describes organic agriculture as "a production system that sustains the health of soils, ecosystems, and people", built on the principles of health, ecology, fairness and care IFOAM 2008. "Regenerative" has no legal or agreed definition at all. One review went through 229 journal articles and 25 practitioner websites and found definitions based on processes (cover crops, livestock integration, less tillage), on outcomes (soil health, carbon, biodiversity), or on a mix of the two. Its authors suggest that anyone using the word should define it for their own context Newton et al. 2020. Some agronomists go further and call it a rebranding of agroecology and sustainable intensification, with soil-carbon claims that are often overstated Giller et al. 2021. "Local" is defined mostly by shoppers and markets. Across 73 studies, local food was not seen as expensive the way organic food is, yet people were still willing to pay more for it Feldmann & Hamm 2015.

Where this leaves my study Draft interpretation

Policy, international and Icelandic, defines food sustainability broadly: security, diets, waste, carbon, organic land share. When it gets to education, it means either consumer information or professional training. Because "regenerative" has no fixed meaning, I will need to define it for the partner farms myself, by what they actually do and what they hope to achieve, and keep it clearly apart from certified organic. That gives me a concrete job. The 2040 food policy and the organic action plan never say what "learning food sustainability" looks like on an Icelandic farm, and that is what I want to find out.

T2Education for sustainability; farm-based, experiential and place-based learning

UNESCO's ESD for 2030 framework sets out what education should do for the SDGs. Its priority areas include policy, learning environments, educators' capacity, and action by young people and at local level UNESCO 2020. Iceland went quite far on paper. The 2011 national curriculum guide made sustainability (sjálfbærni) one of six fundamental pillars (grunnþættir) of education, next to literacy, health and welfare, democracy and human rights, equality, and creativity Mennta- og menningarmálaráðuneytið 2011; Eurydice n.d.. The guide describes education for sustainability as helping people handle questions where environment, society and economy meet Mennta- og menningarmálaráðuneytið 2011. In practice it is patchier. An analysis of the 2011 curriculum found sustainability ideas scattered across school levels and subjects, with the natural sciences, built around action competence, as the clearest exception Jóhannesson 2017. Icelandic researchers also took part in a Nordic mapping of education for sustainability, with Ólafur Páll Jónsson as lead author Jónsson et al. 2021. A recent country report points out that Icelandic school law does not mention sustainability directly. It comes in through the curriculum pillar, and, in the Nordic way, puts weight on students' capacity to act as democratic citizens Pálsdóttir 2025.

For learning on a farm I lean on two traditions, experiential and place-based. Kolb's Experiential Learning builds a whole theory on the idea that people learn through experience, moving round a learning cycle Kolb 2015. Place-based education uses the local community and environment as a "starting place for curriculum learning" Sobel 2005. Gruenewald pushes this towards a "critical pedagogy of place", joining critical pedagogy with ecological place-based education, aiming at both decolonisation and "reinhabitation" Gruenewald 2003. There is not much research on farms specifically, but what exists is encouraging. In a Finnish before-and-after study, 38% of urban pupils had unrealistic ideas about agriculture before a farm visit, and far fewer did afterwards; the authors write that farms "were able to correct pupils' false conceptions" Smeds et al. 2015. In the UK, research on school farm visits looked at how farmers deal with engaging the public and how teachers connect a visit to the curriculum Harris 2009.

Since I am working with young people outside school, I need clear terms for non-formal learning and for participation. The EU–Council of Europe youth partnership glossary calls non-formal learning "purposive but voluntary learning that takes place in a diverse range of environments and situations", where teaching may not even be the main activity EU–CoE Youth Partnership n.d.. The Council of Europe describes youth work as activities "by, with and for young people", resting on "non-formal and informal learning processes" and voluntary participation Council of Europe 2017. Hart argues that young people learn democratic involvement step by step, by doing it, and warns that a lot of participation is only on the surface Hart 1992. His ladder has eight rungs, and the bottom three (manipulation, decoration, tokenism) are not participation at all YMHAC factsheet, adapting Hart 1992. Lundy treats participation as a legal obligation under Article 12 of the UNCRC and breaks it into Space, Voice, Audience and Influence Lundy 2007.

Where this leaves my study Draft interpretation

Iceland's formal commitment is strong: sustainability is a curriculum pillar. The research says schools apply it unevenly, and I found almost nothing linking it to farms or to non-formal settings for people aged 16–30. The farm-visit studies are mostly about children in school and measure short-term knowledge. So my contribution is to take place-based and experiential theory to young adults in a non-formal setting. I will judge the co-design with Lundy's model rather than Hart's ladder, because the question I care about is whether young people actually influence what gets made, not only whether they were asked.

T3Existing farm-education programmes (Nordic and European)

Norway, Inn på tunet (IPT). In Norway, green care means farm-based services for schools and for health and social care. Municipalities pay for most of it, and county agricultural departments advise on quality Haugan et al. 2005. The ministries of education and agriculture wrote a national guide together. It presents approved IPT farms as an addition to the classroom, says farm work should be tied to competence aims in the curriculum, and has the school buy the service from an approved provider Kunnskapsdepartementet et al., n.d.. A small qualitative master's study found that lower-secondary pupils had many experiences of mastery on IPT farms and gained confidence, though their teachers did not see that carry over into school master's thesis 2023. An Erasmus+ Nordic report describes two models: outdoor-focused schools, and private farms hosting pupils through school–municipality partnerships like IPT. It calls the international evidence mixed and asks for more Nordic research Honkajuuri et al. 2021. A review of 98 European green care studies puts Norway (17%) and Sweden (16%) among the leading research countries. Most studies (58%) test therapeutic effect, and few ask about socio-economic or environmental value García-Llorente et al. 2018.

Open-farm events (UK, Estonia). The UK charity LEAF runs Open Farm Sunday. For 2023 it reported about 170,900 visitors, with 88% saying they learned something new about farming and 64% saying they now do things differently LEAF 2023. After the 2017 event LEAF surveyed visitors again about four months later. A quarter replied (26%), and 84% of them said the visit changed how they think about farming. Sponsors include retailers, agribusiness, Defra and AHDB LEAF 2018. I read these numbers with care: they are self-reported and published by the organiser. Estonia's national Open Farm Day started in 2015 with 147 farms and about 45,000 visits. By 2017 there were 280 farms and over 120,000 visits. The Ministry of Rural Affairs organises it with farm organisations, and the money comes from the Rural Development Plan and the EAFRD Ministry of Rural Affairs 2017.

Ireland, social farming. Ireland defines social farming as "offering, on a voluntary basis, farming and horticultural participation in a farming environment". Leitrim Development Company holds the national Social Farming Network contract, paid for by the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine DAFM 2019. In an occupational-therapy pilot in Kildare, 12 of 13 placements were completed. Participants enjoyed it and some went on to jobs or training. Getting to rural farms was the main problem Deegan et al. 2023. Erasmus+ projects. FarmElder, documented by Léargas, is an Erasmus+ adult-education project on social farming with older people Léargas n.d.. InclusiGardens is a small-scale Erasmus+ partnership between Slovenia, Croatia and the Netherlands, making micro-gardening and zero-waste cooking material for disadvantaged adults in cities EPALE n.d..

Iceland. Matís runs Krakkar kokka, an edutainment programme on food, sustainability and nutrition for 11–12-year-olds. It mixes lessons, field trips, cooking and making short documentaries, and was first tried in Skagafjörður in 2018. EIT Food funded the 2020 trial. Afterwards the children were more interested in food and paid more attention to waste and local food Sveinsdóttir et al. 2024. It was later adapted for schools in Portugal with EEA Grants money Sveinsdóttir, Valsdóttir & Geirsdóttir 2024. Reykjavík rents out about 600 allotment plots, and the association Seljagarður runs the old city gardens at Jaðarsel in Breiðholt Reykjavíkurborg 2026, where I have run workshops myself.

Where this leaves my study Draft interpretation

What I found in Europe falls into three groups. Green care and social farming are therapeutic and paid for by municipalities. Open-farm days are one-off events, measured with visitor surveys. School programmes such as IPT and Krakkar kokka are mostly for children. Not one of the programmes I could verify is aimed at 16–30-year-olds, combines farm and digital learning, or is designed together with young people and farmers. Most of the outcome evidence is self-reported or short-term. Iceland has food-education material from Matís and places to grow food in the city, but I could not find a structured farm-education programme for young people. That is the space my study works in.

T4Farm communication, social marketing and social business

Kotler and Zaltman asked whether marketing ideas could be used for social goals, and argued that social causes do better when they borrow marketing's analysis, planning and control Kotler & Zaltman 1971. That is useful to me because it lets me treat a farm's communication as a planned attempt to change behaviour, say food choices or interest in farming, and not only as selling. Yunus and colleagues define a social business as "a no-loss, no-dividend, self-sustaining company that sells goods or services and repays investments to its owners", set up first of all to serve society. From the Grameen experience they take five lessons: question conventional assumptions, partner with organisations that complement you, keep experimenting, find socially minded shareholders, and state the social goals clearly from day one Yunus et al. 2010.

The partner farms sell mostly through short food supply chains (SFSCs). The JRC defines these as chains where food can be traced back to the farmer and there are few or no middlemen: CSA, farm-gate sales, farmers' markets. Most are run by small producers, many of them organic Kneafsey et al. 2013. Consumers like them mainly for quality and for direct contact with the person who grew the food, and the report recommends that Rural Development funding keep paying for marketing and communication training Kneafsey et al. 2013. In Norway, CSA farmers and members share values around environment, justice, health, participation and communication. The farms become places for "converting societal values into practical actions", and the hard part is growing without watering those values down Hvitsand 2016.

Farmers make little use of digital direct marketing. In a survey of 208 German direct marketers, many held back from social media because of time, missing technical skills and not knowing what to post. Honest posts about ordinary farm life did build a connection with customers, yet "many farmers lack a clear social media strategy" Dreist et al. 2025. A Slovak experiment put eight small dairy farms under one shared Facebook brand, and women aged 45–64 on mobile phones turned out to be the most engaged with posts about local products Petriľák et al. 2020.

Where this leaves my study Draft interpretation

Social marketing and social business give me words for what I suspect farm education can be for a small farm: a way to change behaviour and, possibly, something that pays for itself. The SFSC and CSA research shows people value direct contact with farmers, but it looks at that contact as trade, not learning. The few studies of farmers on social media point to missing skills and missing strategy. If young people and farmers make learning content together, the same content could teach and help the farm communicate. I have not found a study that looks at this on Icelandic farms.

T5Digital learning formats

The EU Expert Group on Digitalisation and Youth defines digital youth work as "proactively using or addressing digital media and technology in youth work". It keeps the goals, ethics and principles of youth work in general and can happen face to face, online, or both European Commission 2018. This is close to what I am planning: farm visits plus online material, for young people outside school.

Research on microlearning has grown fast. A bibliometric review counted 476 publications between 2006 and 2019, most of them from e-learning or mobile learning and most set in higher education. In that literature a typical microlearning unit covers one idea and takes no more than about 15 minutes Leong et al. 2021. A PRISMA review of microlearning in higher education (2014–2023) found two design themes, content design and instructional flow Alias & Abdul Razak 2023. The best large-scale evidence on short video I found is an analysis of 6.9 million edX viewing sessions. People watched for a median of about 6 minutes at most, however long the video was, and informal, personal filming held attention better than studio production. The authors recommend cutting videos into pieces under 6 minutes Guo et al. 2014. That suits small farms, which have no film budget.

In agricultural extension, a systematic review of 131 papers follows how ICT has moved extension away from "one-way communication" towards co-innovation and farmers learning from each other. The authors think smartphones and mobile internet could make extension more democratic Khan et al. 2025. Most of the studies are from the Global South, though, so I can only carry it over to Iceland with care.

Where this leaves my study Draft interpretation

The evidence backs short, personal video cut into small pieces, and the idea of digital youth work. But almost all of it comes from universities, MOOCs or extension in the Global South. None of it is about farm-based or sustainability learning for young people. I found no study that joins a farm visit to a digital learning path for young people in the Nordic countries. I can test whether short videos made with farmers keep the learning going between visits, and write down design principles for this mix of farm and online learning.

T6Barriers to entering farming

The EU has worried about generational renewal for a long time. The European Court of Auditors found that EU support for young farmers rests on "a poorly-defined intervention logic, with no expected result and impact specified", and noted that the number of young farmers fell from 3.3 million in 2005 to 2.3 million in 2013 ECA 2017. Under the 2023–27 CAP, farmers under 40 run about 12% of EU holdings. Member States have to set aside at least 3% of direct payments for young farmers, as income support, investment support or start-up aid European Commission n.d.. A 2025 assessment covering all 27 Member States still calls generational renewal "a major challenge". What puts people off, it says, is limited access to land and finance, economic insecurity, poor rural infrastructure and complicated rules EU CAP Network 2025.

Researchers are less sure the problem is framed right. Zagata and Sutherland showed that policy papers mix up "young farmers" and "new entrants", and that Eurostat counts young sole holders while academics mostly study successors. In several countries, Finland among them, their data show no shortage of young farmers at all; shortages show up mainly where small holdings dominate Zagata & Sutherland 2015. The EIP-AGRI Focus Group on new entrants concluded that "there is no single optimal definition of a 'new entrant'", and that new entrants can be any age. In its words, "the most common barrier identified was access to land". Capital came next, then knowledge and skills, networks, markets and housing EIP-AGRI 2016. The group also saw that newcomers are usually outside the traditional farming knowledge networks, and recommended incubators and a louder collective voice for them EIP-AGRI 2016.

In Iceland, the Farmers' Association (Bændasamtök Íslands) told Alþingi in 2023 that "meðalaldur bænda er um 57 ár". It said newcomers find it hard to get in, and pointed to poor access to credit, expensive loans and the need for patient, low-interest capital Bændasamtök Íslands 2023. A panel study of every Icelandic farm from 2000 to 2009 found net entry into sheep and cattle farming was lowest at the edge of the capital region and highest at about 220 km from Reykjavík. Younger people and women were more likely to enter, but, unexpectedly, the young were also more likely to leave Karlsson 2018.

Where this leaves my study Draft interpretation

European and Icelandic sources agree on the main barriers: land, capital, and being outside the knowledge networks. Policy answers mostly with money, through CAP payments and loans. Knowledge and networks are the one barrier an education project can realistically touch. Icelandic farmers are getting older, and young entrants also leave early. I want to see whether learning on a farm, designed together with working regenerative farmers, gives young people who want to farm what EIP-AGRI says newcomers lack: skills, contacts, and an honest picture of what farming involves.

MMethodology: design-based research and thematic analysis

I use design-based research (DBR), which blends "empirical educational research with the theory-driven design of learning environments". The aim is to understand how, when and why an educational innovation works in practice Design-Based Research Collective 2003. It fits because I am building the thing I study. McKenney and Reeves describe educational design research as work done in real settings that tries both to solve practitioners' problems and to produce theory others can use. Their model has four core processes: analysis and exploration, design and construction, evaluation and reflection, and implementation and spread McKenney & Reeves 2019. For the analysis I follow Braun and Clarke, who present thematic analysis as an accessible and theoretically flexible way to find patterns in qualitative data Braun & Clarke 2006. They later renamed it "reflexive TA" and spelled out assumptions the 2006 paper had left unsaid Braun & Clarke 2019. Reflexivity matters in my case, since I am also part of the project I am studying.
