Theory: what the literature says

Six theory chapters plus methodology for the Farm Education for Food Sustainability thesis, written as draft paragraphs with a link to the source after every fact. Full references are on the Sources page.

Every factual sentence below links to its source. All sources are listed on the Sources page.

T1Sustainability, narrowed to food sustainability

The most common definition of sustainability comes from the Brundtland report: development that “meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs” (World Commission on Environment and Development [WCED], 1987, p. 43). It has three sides: environmental, social and economic. Food is where all three meet. Farming is a large part of the environmental side: food systems caused about a third (34%) of global greenhouse gas emissions in 2015 (Crippa et al., 2021).

The usual definition of food security comes from the 1996 World Food Summit: “food security exists when all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient” safe and nutritious food that meets their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations [FAO], 1996). Food systems thinking looks wider. FAO uses the definition of its High Level Panel of Experts, in which a sustainable food system “delivers food security and nutrition for all” without harming the economic, social and environmental bases that future generations need (FAO, 2018; FAO, n.d.).

The same idea applies to diets. In 2010, an international symposium at FAO in Rome agreed that sustainable diets have low environmental impacts and are “protective and respectful of biodiversity and ecosystems, culturally acceptable”, affordable, nutritionally adequate, safe and healthy (Burlingame & Dernini, 2012). The EAT-Lancet Commission later put numbers on it. To feed about 10 billion people by 2050, it says, diets, production and food waste all have to change. Red meat and sugar should be cut by more than half, and nuts, fruits, vegetables and legumes more than doubled (Stockholm Resilience Centre, 2019; Willett et al., 2019).

These goals sit in the 2030 Agenda, adopted by the UN General Assembly on 25 September 2015 (United Nations, 2015). Two targets under SDG 12 matter here. Target 12.3 is to “halve per capita global food waste at the retail and consumer levels” by 2030. Target 12.8 is to make sure people everywhere have “the relevant information and awareness” for sustainable development and lifestyles (United Nations Regional Information Centre [UNRIC], n.d.). Target 12.8 is the direct link between food sustainability and education.

Iceland has its own food policy to 2040 (Matvælastefna Íslands til ársins 2040), published in June 2023. It has six focus areas: sustainable food production, society, food security, food safety, consumer needs, and research, innovation and education. Its vision includes carbon-neutral food production (Matvælaráðuneytið, 2023). Alþingi adopted it as a parliamentary resolution (Alþingi, 2023a). A first action plan for organic production was presented as a draft in November 2023 (Stjórnarráð Íslands, 2023), and the final version followed in August 2024. Its goal is that “10% af landbúnaðarlandi á Íslandi verði komið með lífræna vottun 2040” [10% of agricultural land in Iceland will be certified organic by 2040]. It includes actions on consumer education and training for producers (Matvælaráðuneytið, 2024). The European Environment Agency repeats the 10% target and notes that Iceland’s organic area “has shown no growth” (European Environment Agency [EEA], 2025). For comparison, the EU’s Farm to Fork strategy aims for at least 25% organic land by 2030 (Stjórnarráð Íslands, 2023).

Three labels are often mixed up, and they mean different things. Organic is a legal, certified category. In the EU it is governed by Regulation (EU) 2018/848, which requires checks at every stage and makes the EU logo compulsory on packaged organic food (Regulation (EU) 2018/848, 2018). IFOAM describes organic agriculture as “a production system that sustains the health of soils, ecosystems, and people”, based on four principles: health, ecology, fairness and care (IFOAM, 2008).

Regenerative agriculture has no legal or agreed definition. A review of 229 journal articles and 25 practitioner websites found definitions based on processes (such as cover crops, integrating livestock and reduced tillage), on outcomes (such as soil health, carbon storage and biodiversity), or on both. The authors recommend that everyone using the term defines it for their own context (Newton et al., 2020). Some agronomists see it as a new name for agroecology and sustainable intensification, and argue that its claims about soil carbon are often exaggerated (Giller et al., 2021).

Local food is defined mostly by consumers and markets. A review of 73 studies found that, unlike organic food, local food is not seen as expensive, yet people are still willing to pay more for it (Feldmann & Hamm, 2015). All three aim at sustainability, in different ways. This study uses them side by side and teaches the difference.

T2Education for sustainability; farm-based, experiential and place-based learning

Education for sustainability. UNESCO’s framework ESD for 2030 sets out how education should help reach the SDGs. It names priority action areas, including policy, learning environments, educators, and action by young people and at local level (United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization [UNESCO], 2020).

In Iceland, sustainability (sjálfbærni) became one of the six fundamental pillars (grunnþættir) of education in the 2011 national curriculum guide. The other five are literacy, health and welfare, democracy and human rights, equality, and creativity (Eurydice, n.d.; Mennta- og menningarmálaráðuneytið, 2011). The curriculum describes education for sustainability as helping people deal with issues where environment, society and economy meet (Mennta- og menningarmálaráðuneytið, 2011). In practice, the picture is mixed. An analysis of the 2011 curriculum found that ideas about sustainability are mostly scattered across school levels and subjects. The clearest exception is the natural sciences, which are built around action competence (Jóhannesson, 2017). A Nordic mapping of education for sustainability, led by Ólafur Páll Jónsson, included Icelandic researchers (Jónsson et al., 2021). A recent country report notes that Icelandic school laws do not mention sustainability directly, but that it is built into the curriculum through the pillar. In the Nordic tradition, the curriculum stresses students’ ability to act as democratic citizens (Pálsdóttir, 2025).

Learning on farms: experience and place. Learning on a farm draws on two traditions. The first is experiential learning. Kolb’s theory is that people learn through experience, in a cycle of doing, reflecting, thinking and trying again (Kolb, 2015). The second is place-based education, which uses the local community and environment as a “starting place for curriculum learning” (Sobel, 2005). Gruenewald (2003) goes further with a “critical pedagogy of place”. It combines critical pedagogy with ecological place-based education, with two aims: decolonisation and “reinhabitation”, learning to live well in a place again.

Research on farm learning itself is still small, but encouraging. In a Finnish study before and after a farm visit, 38% of urban pupils had unrealistic ideas about agriculture beforehand, and this share dropped significantly afterwards. The authors conclude that the farms “were able to correct pupils’ false conceptions” (Smeds et al., 2015). Research in the UK on school visits to farms looked at how farmers deal with engaging the public, and how teachers link visits to the curriculum (Harris, 2009).

Learning outside school, and taking part. This study works with the general public and young people outside school, so two ideas matter: non-formal learning and participation. The EU–Council of Europe youth partnership defines non-formal learning as “purposive but voluntary learning that takes place in a diverse range of environments and situations”, where teaching and learning are not necessarily the main activity (EU–Council of Europe Youth Partnership, n.d.). The Council of Europe (2017) describes youth work as activities “by, with and for young people”, based on “non-formal and informal learning processes” and on voluntary participation.

Participation can be real or only for show. Hart (1992) argues that young people build the skills for democratic involvement step by step, through practice, and warns that participation is often superficial. His ladder of participation has eight rungs. The lowest three (manipulation, decoration and tokenism) are not real participation (Youth Mental Health Advisory Council [YMHAC], n.d.). Lundy (2007) sees participation as a right under Article 12 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, with four elements: space, voice, audience and influence.

T3Existing programmes: Iceland and abroad

This chapter gives a short overview. The full scan of programmes, with sources, is in Appendix [X] (Examples of farming education in Northern Europe, checked 30 October 2026).

Iceland

Formal agricultural education in Iceland is mainly at the Agricultural University of Iceland (LbhÍ) at Hvanneyri, and at the horticultural school at Reykir, run by Fjölbrautaskóli Suðurlands (FSu) since 2022. Reykir has an organic horticulture track, including a 6-credit course on growing organic outdoor vegetables (LÍFR2ÚT06). It covers seed, soil, fertility, plant protection, harvest and marketing, and includes a field trip to the Reykjalundur nursery in Grímsnes (Fjölbrautaskóli Suðurlands, 2025). Hallormsstaðaskóli offers a one- to two-year programme in creative sustainability, which moved to University of Iceland diploma level, under the School of Education, from autumn 2025 (Stjórnarráð Íslands, 2024). These routes suit people who have already decided to study for a year or more.

Outside formal education, the offer is small, and much of it has stopped. The closest current match is Gróandi in Ísafjörður: a community-supported agriculture (CSA) farm with permaculture events and courses, which also hosts a European Solidarity Corps volunteer placement (European Youth Portal, 2026). Its founder has taught a five-day introduction to permaculture, in English, at Hallormsstaðaskóli (Visit East Iceland, 2023). A full 72-hour Permaculture Design Course was held at Alviðra in 2014 (Náttúran.is, 2014), but no later one was found. SEEDS Iceland ran volunteer workcamps on certified organic farms in East and West Iceland in 2014–2015 (SEEDS Iceland, 2015). Its current calls include no farming. Sólheimar eco-village hosted organic growing, seminars and EU youth programmes for many years (SALTO-YOUTH, n.d.). Its centre, Sesseljuhús, is now presented as a history museum, with no courses listed (South Iceland, n.d.). For farmers themselves, Loftslagsvænn landbúnaður (Climate-Friendly Agriculture) has run since 2020. Farmers visit host farms in on-farm workshops and attend monthly online lectures, with 55 farms taking part in early 2025 (Land og skógur, 2025). It is peer learning between farmers, not open to the public.

Most public learning about food growing happens at one-off events and through farmers’ own sales channels. Examples are the Rætur framtíðar symposium on food security and regenerative land use at Hvanneyri (Land og skógur, 2026), the Slow Food Reykjavík festival BragðaGarður, with talks and a producer market, and a children’s sowing workshop at the Reykjavík Botanic Garden (Reykjavíkurborg, 2025). Farmers also reach the public through REKO Reykjavík (a Facebook farmers’ market) and vegetable-box subscriptions, such as Reykjalundur’s (Reykjavík Grapevine, 2025). These are digital channels without a learning layer. The only Icelandic-language video resource on organic growing found was RÚV’s archive series Grænir fingur (1989–1990), republished online in 2021 (RÚV, 2021).

Matís runs Krakkar kokka, a programme that teaches children aged 11–12 about food, sustainability and nutrition in a fun way. It combines lessons, field trips, cooking and making short documentary films. It was first tried in Skagafjörður in 2018, and the 2020 trial was funded through EIT Food. Afterwards, the children were more interested in food topics and paid more attention to food waste and local food (Sveinsdóttir, Guðlaugsdóttir, et al., 2024). The programme was later adapted for schools in Portugal with EEA Grants funding (Sveinsdóttir, Valsdóttir, & Geirsdóttir, 2024). In Reykjavík, the city rents out about 600 allotment plots, and the association Seljagarður runs the former city gardens at Jaðarsel in Breiðholt (Reykjavíkurborg, 2026). Seljagarður has run a growing course (2020) and holds educational days (Seljagarður, n.d.).

No current WWOOF network, no recent full permaculture design course, no LbhÍ short courses on organic growing, no active open-farm network (Opinn landbúnaður), no education programme from the organic growers’ association VOR or the young farmers’ association, and no Erasmus+ youth exchanges on regenerative farming hosted in Iceland. No Icelandic programme was found that combines farm visits with digital learning, or that is co-designed with its learners.

Norway: Inn på tunet. In Norway, “green care” means farm-based services for schools and for health and social care. Most of these services are paid for by the municipality, and county agricultural offices advise on quality (Haugan et al., 2005). A national guide from the ministries of education and agriculture presents approved Inn på tunet farms as an extra learning space next to the classroom. Farm work should be linked to aims in the national curriculum, and the school buys the service from an approved farm (Kunnskapsdepartementet et al., n.d.). A small qualitative master’s study found that lower-secondary pupils often felt they mastered tasks on these farms, and grew in self-confidence. Their teachers, however, did not see this carry over to school (Gården som mestringsarena, 2023). A Nordic Erasmus+ report describes two models: schools focused on outdoor learning, and private farms that host pupils through partnerships between schools and municipalities, such as Inn på tunet. It notes that international research on outcomes is mixed, and calls for more Nordic research (Honkajuuri et al., 2021). A review of 98 European green care studies found Norway (17%) and Sweden (16%) among the leading countries. Most studies (58%) measure therapeutic effects, and few look at social, economic or environmental value (García-Llorente et al., 2018).

Open farm days: the UK and Estonia. In the UK, the charity LEAF runs Open Farm Sunday. In 2023 it reported about 170,900 visitors. In its surveys, 88% of visitors said they learned something new about farming, and 64% said they now do things differently (Linking Environment And Farming [LEAF], 2023). In a follow-up survey about four months after the 2017 event (26% response rate), 84% said the visit changed how they think about farming. Sponsors include retailers, agribusiness companies, Defra and AHDB (LEAF, 2018). These figures are self-reported and published by the organiser, so they should be read with care. Estonia’s national Open Farm Day started in 2015 with 147 farms and about 45,000 visits. By 2017 it had 280 farms and more than 120,000 visits. It is organised by the Ministry of Rural Affairs with farmers’ organisations, and funded through the Rural Development Plan and the EU’s rural development fund, EAFRD (Ministry of Rural Affairs of the Republic of Estonia, 2017).

Ireland: social farming. Ireland defines social farming as “offering, on a voluntary basis, farming and horticultural participation in a farming environment”. Leitrim Development Company runs the national Social Farming Network, funded by the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine (Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine [DAFM], 2019). An occupational therapy pilot in Kildare completed 12 of 13 farm placements. Participants said they enjoyed it, and some moved on to jobs or training. Getting to rural farms was a barrier (Deegan et al., 2023).

Erasmus+ projects. FarmElder is an Erasmus+ adult education project on social farming for older people (Léargas, n.d.). InclusiGardens is a small Erasmus+ partnership (Slovenia, Croatia and the Netherlands) that develops resources on micro-gardening and zero-waste cooking for disadvantaged adults in cities (EPALE, n.d.).

T4Farm communication, social marketing and social business

Social marketing. Kotler and Zaltman asked whether marketing ideas can be used for social goals. They argued that social causes move forward better when they use marketing analysis, planning and control (Kotler & Zaltman, 1971). For farm learning, this means communication is a planned effort to change behaviour, such as food choices or interest in farming, and not only a way to sell.

Social business. Yunus and colleagues define a social business as “a no-loss, no-dividend, self-sustaining company that sells goods or services and repays investments to its owners”, whose main purpose is to serve society. From the Grameen experience they draw five lessons: challenge conventional assumptions, partner with organisations that complement you, experiment all the time, recruit socially minded shareholders, and state social goals clearly from the start (Yunus et al., 2010).

Short food supply chains. Short food supply chains are the market form closest to the partner farms. The EU’s Joint Research Centre defines them as chains where food can be traced back to the farmer, with few or no intermediaries. Examples are community supported agriculture (CSA), farm-gate sales and farmers’ markets, and most are run by small producers, often organic. Consumers value them mainly for product quality and direct contact with producers, and the report recommends continued rural development funding for training in marketing and communication (Kneafsey et al., 2013). In Norway, CSA farmers and members share values around the environment, justice, health, participation and communication. The farms work as places for “converting societal values into practical actions”, and the main challenge is to grow without losing those values (Hvitsand, 2016). Both partner farms in this study sell through vegetable-box subscriptions, so this is directly relevant.

Farms online. Farmers use digital direct marketing less than they could. A survey of 208 German farmers who sell directly found that many hold back from social media because of limited time, lack of technical skills and uncertainty about what to post. Honest posts about everyday farm life strengthened the connection with customers, yet “many farmers lack a clear social media strategy” (Dreist et al., 2025). A Slovak experiment promoted eight small dairy farms under one shared Facebook brand. Women aged 45–64 using mobile phones were the group most engaged with posts about local products (Petriľák et al., 2020).

T5Digital learning formats

Digital youth work. The EU Expert Group on Digitalisation and Youth defines digital youth work as “proactively using or addressing digital media and technology in youth work”. It has the same goals, ethics and principles as youth work in general, and can happen face to face, online or both (European Commission, 2018). This fits a project that mixes farm visits with digital material for people outside formal schooling.

Microlearning. Microlearning is a fast-growing research topic. A review found 476 publications from 2006 to 2019, mostly about e-learning or mobile learning, and mostly in higher education. Typical microlearning focuses on one idea and lasts no more than about 15 minutes (Leong et al., 2021). A systematic review of microlearning in higher education (2014–2023) found two design themes: how the content is designed, and how the teaching flows (Alias & Abdul Razak, 2023).

Short video. The strongest large-scale evidence comes from 6.9 million viewing sessions on edX. Whatever the length of a video, people watched for about 6 minutes at most (median). Informal, personal filming worked better than studio production. The authors recommend cutting videos into parts shorter than 6 minutes (Guo et al., 2014). For small farms, this is good news: short, simple videos filmed on the farm are what works.

Digital tools in farm advice. A systematic review of 131 papers on agricultural extension shows how digital tools moved advice from “one-way communication” towards learning together and farmer-to-farmer learning. The authors suggest that smartphones and mobile internet could make extension more democratic (Khan et al., 2025). The review focuses on the Global South, so it applies to Iceland only in part.

T6Barriers to entering farming

The EU picture. Getting new generations into farming has worried the EU for a long time. The European Court of Auditors found that EU support for young farmers rests on “a poorly-defined intervention logic, with no expected result and impact specified”. It also noted that the number of young farmers fell from 3.3 million in 2005 to 2.3 million in 2013 (European Court of Auditors, 2017). Under the 2023–2027 Common Agricultural Policy, farmers under 40 manage about 12% of EU farms. Member States must set aside at least 3% of direct payments for young farmers, as income support, investment support or start-up aid (European Commission, n.d.). A 2025 assessment of all 27 Member States still calls generational renewal “a major challenge”. The main reasons people stay away are limited access to land and finance, economic insecurity, weak rural infrastructure and complex rules (EU CAP Network, 2025).

Young farmers or new entrants? Researchers question how the problem is framed. Zagata and Sutherland (2015) showed that policy documents mix up “young farmers” and “new entrants”, and that Eurostat counts young sole holders while research focuses on successors. Their data show no shortage of young farmers in several countries, including Finland. Shortages appear mainly where small farms dominate. The EIP-AGRI Focus Group on new entrants concluded that “there is no single optimal definition of a ‘new entrant’”, and that new entrants can be any age. It found that “the most common barrier identified was access to land”, followed by money, knowledge and skills, networks, markets and housing. Newcomers are usually cut off from traditional farming knowledge networks, so the group recommended incubators and a stronger shared voice for new entrants (EIP-AGRI Focus Group, 2016).

Iceland. The Farmers’ Association of Iceland (Bændasamtök Íslands) told Alþingi in 2023 that “meðalaldur bænda er um 57 ár” [the average age of farmers is about 57]. It said that it is hard for newcomers to get into farming, and pointed to limited access to credit, expensive loans and the need for patient, low-interest capital (Bændasamtök Íslands, 2023). A study of all Icelandic farms from 2000 to 2009 found that new sheep and cattle farms were least likely at the edge of the capital region, and most likely about 220 km from Reykjavík. More younger people and women started farming, but, unexpectedly, young people were also more likely to stop (Karlsson, 2018).

MMethodology: staged co-design, case study and thematic analysis

Co-design in stages. This study is the first stage of a co-design process. In co-design, the people a design is for take part in shaping it. Sanders and Stappers describe the early “front end” of co-design, where participants help to explore the problem and frame what should be made, before anything is built (Sanders & Stappers, 2008). The Design Council’s Double Diamond describes the same early work as two steps, discover and define (Design Council, n.d.). This study does both: it finds out what each group wants and what the farms can offer, and turns this into a co-designed map of learning opportunities for the general public and young people. Each group decides once, in sequence, and the answers are combined by a stated rule. Iteration comes in a later stage.

Links to design-based research. The approach is close to design-based research, which combines “empirical educational research with the theory-driven design of learning environments” and asks how, when and why educational innovations work in practice (Design-Based Research Collective, 2003). McKenney and Reeves describe educational design research as research in real settings that aims both to solve practical problems and to build usable theory. Their model has four core processes: analysis and exploration, design and construction, evaluation and reflection, and implementation and spread (McKenney & Reeves, 2019). This study covers the first of these, analysis and exploration.

Cases and sample size. The three sites are studied as a multiple case study, chosen to be different from each other: an urban community garden, a regenerative farm without organic certification, and a certified organic and biodynamic farm. With few participants, the sample is justified by information power: a narrow aim, participants who know exactly what is being asked about, and in-depth conversations mean that fewer participants are needed (Malterud et al., 2016).

Analysis. Survey and polling answers are described with simple statistics and compared across the groups. Interviews and session notes are analysed with thematic analysis, an accessible and theoretically flexible method for finding patterns in qualitative data (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Braun and Clarke later renamed their approach reflexive thematic analysis, and made clear assumptions that the 2006 paper had left unsaid (Braun & Clarke, 2019). Findings are reported per site.
