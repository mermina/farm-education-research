Farm Education Research

How can young people learn food sustainability from real farms, on the farm and online? A design-based study co-creating learning materials with farmers and young people in Iceland, run with Green Wellbeing. Research design draft, October 2026.

R1Working title and main question

Learning food sustainability on regenerative farms: co-designing farm-based and digital learning with farmers and young people in Iceland

Main question: How can learning materials co-designed by farmers and young people, both on the farm and digital, engage youth in food sustainability (local, organic and regenerative food) and open a path for those who want to farm?

  1. Landscape: what farm-based and food-sustainability education exists in Iceland and abroad, formal, non-formal and informal, and where are the gaps, especially for young people who want to enter farming?
  2. The visit: what do young people (both groups) and farmers value in hands-on farm learning, and how should materials be shaped around it?
  3. Digital: how do the partner farms communicate now, and which co-designed digital formats carry learning beyond the visit while also supporting the farm's sales?

Built into the interviews, not a separate question: what young people see as barriers to entering farming (land, money, knowledge, not knowing where to start). This feeds a short policy note and future research.

R2Audiences

Audience 1

Learning food sustainability

Young people and the wider public who learn about sustainability, and food sustainability in particular, through real local, organic and regenerative farms.

Audience 2

Learning to farm

Young people who want the actual practices, including those who might farm professionally.

Secondary

Farmers

Co-designers of the materials, and learners about communicating what they do.

The “harbour”: young people in Iceland, Poland and Europe don't know where to go and where to start. The long-term output is one place with useful links in the sector and clear paths into food growing and farming, starting where you live: a balcony, a community garden such as Seljagarður, an allotment, then a real farm.

R3Theory chapters

  • T1
    Sustainability, narrowed to food sustainability
    Food systems, food security, SDG 2 and 12, Iceland's food policy to 2040. Definitions: organic is certification, regenerative is a set of practices with no fixed standard, local is about distance. The project teaches the difference.
    Literature
  • T2
    Education in all its forms
    Formal (school and upper-secondary curricula; adult and vocational education such as the Agricultural University of Iceland and Garðyrkjuskólinn á Reykjum), non-formal (Erasmus+, NGO projects, Climate Café Iceland), informal (libraries, community gardens, farm visits). Education for sustainability, place-based and experiential learning, youth participation as democratic practice.
    Literature
  • T3
    Existing programmes: Iceland first, mainly abroad
    Research what is available first: how they operate, what they offer, who funds them. Start with LEAF Open Farm Sunday, Estonia's Open Farm Day, Poland's educational farms, InclusiGardens, FarmElder, Inn på tunet, care farms, Social Farming Ireland, Farm School NYC, Stone Barns, Glynwood, Matís's Krakkar kokka. Include urban and community gardening programmes (e.g. Seljagarður in Reykjavík).
    Literature
  • T4
    How farms communicate: social marketing and social business
    Stated openly: if educational content also raises sales, that is the symbiosis we want. Many farmers see communication as wasted money and take part only with a grant; showing that the same content educates and sells makes them willing to invest. Social marketing (Kotler and Zaltman, 1971); small farms as social businesses.
    Literature
  • T5
    Digital learning formats
    Short video and microlearning, online learning paths, digital youth work; what small farms can keep up with little time.
    Literature
  • T6
    Barriers to entering farming
    Land access, capital, subsidies, generational renewal in Iceland and Europe. Context, and the basis for the policy note.
    Literature

R4Method: design-based research

PhaseWhenWhat
0 LandscapeNov 2026 – Apr 2027Literature T1–T6; review of existing programmes
1 BaselineSpring 2027How the partner farms communicate now; sales numbers if they agree to share
2 Co-designMay – June 2027Workshops with farmers and young people
3 TestJune – Aug 2027Farm visits plus 2–3 digital formats
4 EvaluateAug – Sep 2027Interviews, short survey, digital engagement before and after

One summer shows whether the approach is promising, not long-term proof. Interviews and surveys follow University of Iceland ethics approval.
