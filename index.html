Farm Education Research

How can people of all ages, especially young people, learn about food sustainability from real farms, both on the farm and digitally? In my master's research I will find out what the public, young people and farmers want from this kind of learning, make a short course together with them (a few short videos recorded with farmers, plus selected reading and links), get feedback from learners, and outline a pathway into farming for those who want to go further. This is my draft research design from October 2026.

★What this study is about, and what is new

Why food. Food is one of our most basic human needs: without it there is no health, no learning and no life. The right to food is part of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Article 25), and ending hunger and making farming sustainable is one of the UN’s Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 2). Almost all of our food starts on a farm. Sustainability means meeting the needs of today without taking away the ability of future generations to meet theirs, and food is the clearest example: if the way we farm wears out the soil, water and climate that farming depends on, we put our most basic need at risk.

The problem. And today, the way we produce food is one of the biggest pressures on the planet. Agrifood systems cause about one third of all greenhouse gas emissions caused by people (32% in 2023). Food systems also cause 80% of deforestation, use 70% of the world’s fresh water and are the largest cause of biodiversity loss on land, and up to 40% of the Earth’s land is already degraded. How we farm, and what we choose to eat, is part of the solution.

Iceland knows this problem well. Since settlement, woodland loss and grazing have led to soil erosion that is among the worst known outside the world’s drought regions. Food security is a concern too: Iceland grows about 43% of the vegetables it eats, and its farms depend on imported fuel and fertiliser.

The government is moving. Iceland now has a Food Policy to 2040, an Agricultural Policy to 2040 that calls for direct links between producers and consumers, and an organic action plan with a target of 10% organic farmland by 2040. But none of their action plans includes learning for the general public, schools or farm visits.

Why everyone needs this, and who it is for. Food concerns all of us. Every day we choose what to buy, cook and throw away, and as citizens we help decide how food is produced. Research links food literacy (understanding where food comes from and how it is produced) with more sustainable diets and less food waste, although knowledge alone is not enough: people also need real experience. Seeing a working farm helps. In Finland, a single farm visit significantly reduced urban pupils’ wrong ideas about farming. So this learning is for everyone in Iceland, of all ages: to make informed everyday choices, and to take part in decisions about our food system.

It is also about who will farm next. In the EU, only about 12% of farm managers were under 40 in 2020. In Iceland, research describes a continuous decline in the number of farmers and weakening rural communities. Interest in farming has to start somewhere, usually long before anyone is ready to commit to years of formal study at the Agricultural University or the horticulture school at Reykir. Most people are busy with jobs, studies and families, the information is scattered, and learning directly from farmers is rare. An easy first step, on a farm or with digital tools at your own pace, can grow that interest into a real choice.

What is missing. I mapped what exists in Iceland. No scheme pays farmers to host the public. The open-farm list has had no update since 2020. Matís’s farm-visit and video project Krakkar kokka ended in 2024. No platform brings Icelandic farmers’ knowledge together in one place for people to learn at their own pace. Other countries, such as Bavaria and England, pay farms for each educational visit.

What this study brings. An easy way into sustainable food and farming, made of two parts that work together: learning on the farm, through visits where people see, touch and ask about soil, compost, crop diversity and short supply chains; and a modern digital platform for learning at your own pace, with short videos from Icelandic farmers, clear explanations of organic, regenerative and agroecological farming, and links to take the next step. Both are designed together with farmers and young people, and both are meant to be useful to the farms themselves. As far as I could find, nothing in Iceland or the other Nordic countries combines these parts.

The focus is the sustainable side of farming, organic or not, which I describe with FAO’s agroecology framework. Some farms that use good local compost cannot be certified, because Iceland’s organic action plan names the shortage of permitted fertilisers as the main obstacle. The study does not compare farming systems; it asks how this kind of learning can be designed.

R1Working title and research questions

Learning food sustainability from Icelandic farms: co-designing farm visits and a digital platform with farmers and young people

Main question: How can farmers, young people and the wider public together create accessible learning about food sustainability through sustainable farming practices, on Icelandic farms and with digital tools, and what would farmers need to offer both an open course for everyone and a pathway into farming for young people who want to farm?

  1. SQ1 What exists: what learning about sustainable food and farming exists in Iceland and abroad, on farms and with digital tools, both easy-access learning for everyone and pathways into farming, and where are the gaps?
  2. SQ2 What each group wants and needs: what do the general public, young people who want to farm, and farmers value in learning about farming and food, on the farm and digitally? What stands in the way, and what support would make it possible?
  3. SQ3 The platform: how can a learning platform, co-designed with farmers and young people, support learning about food sustainability, and what should a pathway into farming in Iceland include?
Track A: an open course for everyoneTrack B: a pathway into farming
WhoThe general public and young people (16–30) who are curious about foodYoung people who want to grow food or become farmers

Farm visits in summer 2027 if funding allows. Feedback from learners.Explore what exists abroad and what Icelandic farmers would be willing and able to offer; draft the steps of a pathway and check them with farmers and young people

I can't build and test a full course for future farmers in one summer, so Track B stays at the first stage of design-based research: analysis and exploration (McKenney & Reeves, 2019). The questions about barriers sit inside SQ2 and feed both the policy note and the Track B design brief.

R2Audiences

Group 1

Learning food sustainability

The general public and young people who are curious about where food comes from and how it is grown on real local, organic and regenerative farms.

Group 2

Learning to farm

Young people who want to grow food or might farm professionally, but are not on a formal agricultural course or don't know where to start.

Group 3

Farmers

Hosts and co-designers of the learning. I also ask what education could mean for them and their farm.

The “harbour” becomes the platform: young people in Iceland and the Nordic countries often have no idea where to go or where to start. In the long run I want one place that collects the useful links and shows clear routes into growing food and farming, starting from where you live: a balcony, a community garden like Seljagarður, an allotment, short courses, a farm placement, and then land and money for a farm of your own. The platform built in this study is its first version.

R3Theory chapters

Read the full theory chapters with sources →

  • T1
    Sustainability, narrowed to food sustainability
    Food systems, food security, SDG 2 and 12, Iceland's food policy to 2040. Organic is a certification, regenerative is a set of practices with no fixed standard, agroecological covers sustainable practices whether certified or not (FAO), and local is about distance. The project teaches the difference.
    Literature
  • T2
    Education in all its forms
    Formal (school and upper-secondary curricula; adult and vocational education such as the Agricultural University of Iceland and Garðyrkjuskólinn á Reykjum), non-formal (Erasmus+, NGO projects, Climate Café Iceland), informal (libraries, community gardens, farm visits). Education for sustainability, place-based and experiential learning, youth participation as democratic practice.
    Literature
  • T3
    Existing programmes, Iceland first
    I look at what already exists first: how programmes run, what they offer and who pays for them. Iceland comes first, then the other Nordic countries, plus a few examples from the rest of Europe. I start with Inn på tunet (Norway), Nordic care farms and open-farm days, Matís's Krakkar kokka, LEAF Open Farm Sunday (UK), Estonia's Open Farm Day, InclusiGardens, FarmElder and Social Farming Ireland. I also include urban and community gardening, such as Seljagarður in Reykjavík, and non-formal learning on sustainable growing in Iceland and the Nordics: permaculture courses, folk high school farm lines and EU youth volunteering on farms.
    Literature
  • T4
    How farms communicate: social marketing and social business
    I say this openly: if content that teaches also helps a farm sell, that is exactly the win-win I am hoping for. Many farmers think of communication as money down the drain and only join in when there is a grant. If they can see the same content teaching and selling, they are more likely to put time into it. Social marketing (Kotler and Zaltman, 1971); small farms as social businesses.
    Literature
  • T5
    Digital learning formats
    Short video and microlearning, digital learning paths people use at their own pace and time, digital youth work; what small farms can keep up with little time.
    Literature
  • T6
    Barriers to entering farming
    Land, capital, subsidies and generational renewal in Iceland and Europe. This is background, and the basis for the policy note.
    Literature

I will not collect sales figures. Whether education can also help a farm (visibility, relationships with customers, direct sales) is explored through the literature and through what farmers tell me. The study does not try to prove that the course works. Learners' feedback is used to improve the design (formative evaluation), which is what design-based research asks for. Surveys and interviews will only start after University of Iceland ethics approval.

R4bThe practical research, step by step

The fieldwork happens on partner farms (in South and North-East Iceland, still to be confirmed), on digital tools, and in Reykjavík: a community growing plot we use as an open “playground” for trying things out, and a community garden for the co-design session.

Who takes part

General public and curious young people

An online survey, aiming for 50 or more answers, shared through youth centres, upper-secondary schools, universities, libraries and my own networks. About 10–15 people then try the course and give feedback; more if farm visits are funded.

Who takes part

Young people who want to farm

About 6–10 people, in one focus group or individual interviews, and again in the feedback round on the pathway outline.

Who takes part

Farmers and growers

Interviews with 4–6 farmers or growers. 2–3 of them help choose the topics and record a video with me, and they are paid for their time. If funding comes through, they also host farm visits in summer 2027.

  1. Feb – May 2027
    1 · Needs assessment (SQ2)
    • Online survey of the general public and curious young people: interest, preferred formats, topics, barriers.
    • Focus group or interviews with young people who want to farm: interest, what they need to learn, what stops them, what support would help.
    • Farmer interviews: what they would like to share about their farming, how much time they could give, and how they run things; how they teach and communicate now, what they could offer and to whom, their barriers and needs, whether education could also help the farm. Each farmer reacts to 4–5 examples from abroad: "Which of these could you imagine hosting?"
    • A short look at each farm's website and social media before the interview.
  2. May – June
    2 · Co-design round
    • From the interviews I draft 5–6 possible topics and a rough outline for each video.
    • Farmers and young people choose, change and add, in one short session together or in an online round. They decide together what goes into the course.
    • We also sketch the first steps of the pathway into farming.
  3. June – August
    3 · Make and try out the course (platform level 1)
    • Record 2–3 short videos (under 6 minutes each) with farmers: for example soil and compost, why they farm sustainably, a day on the farm. Each farmer approves the final cut.
    • Collect and organise reading and links for the theory part: what organic, regenerative, agroecological and local mean, and food systems and sustainability.
    • Put everything on a simple digital learning path that people can use at their own pace, when and where it suits them.
    • If funding comes through: farm visits in summer 2027, where the same farms host the public, with a farm walk, a hands-on task and a short reflection.
  4. Aug – Sep
    4 · Feedback and check
    • 10–15 learners use the course and answer a short questionnaire: what was clear, what was useful, what was missing, and what they would now like to do (grow food, visit a farm, buy locally).
    • Short follow-up conversations with a few learners and with the farmers.
    • Observation notes from farm visits, if they happen.
    • Feedback round on the pathway outline (platform level 2) with farmers and young people who want to farm.

Analysis: I will use thematic analysis for the interviews, focus group and notes, and simple descriptive statistics for the survey and questionnaires. In design-based research you revise the materials after each round, so what comes out is the materials themselves plus a set of design principles that have been tested in practice.

Ethics: University of Iceland approval, informed consent from everyone, parental consent for anyone under 18, separate consent for filming, an anonymous survey, and no names attached to quotes.

R5What it produces

Platform level 1

Short course on food sustainability

2–3 short videos made with farmers, plus selected reading and links, in one digital learning path that people use at their own pace, improved with learners' feedback. Farm visits are added if funding comes through.

Platform level 2

Pathway into farming, outline

The steps and chapters of a route into farming in Iceland, checked with farmers and young people: the harbour, version 1.

Output

Design brief for a future course

What a longer course for future farmers would need, which farms could host it and what support they would need: the basis for a grant application.

Output

Design principles

Tested principles for farm-based and digital learning made together with farmers and young people.

Output

Proposal: open farms for learning

A simple, ongoing grant that pays farms a fixed fee for hosting the public a few times a year, with each farm's short video on one shared platform. Iceland has no such scheme; Austria, Bavaria, England and Finland do.

Output

Policy note

What keeps young people out of farming (land, money, knowledge, support), written for government and for future research.

R7About the researcher

I'm Marina Ermina, a mixed-methods researcher and educator. My work sits where human wellbeing, sustainability and learning meet. I hold a first-class MA in Environment and Natural Resources from the University of Iceland (2025), and this study is the thesis for my second MA, in International Education.

In my earlier research I looked at how human wellbeing and ecological sustainability are measured in the Nordic countries and how the two connect. I compared wellbeing indices (HDI, SPI, SDG Index) with the Ecological Footprint and mapped national wellbeing-economy frameworks. I have also worked as a data analyst on the National Footprint and Biocapacity Accounts.

I co-founded Green Wellbeing, and through it I have run environmental and wellbeing workshops, including at Seljagarður community garden, SEEDS Iceland and GAIA Iceland. I have worked with children as a nature and wellbeing educator and done Erasmus+ training for youth trainers. I also grow food myself and work alongside regenerative farming in Iceland.
