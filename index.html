Farm Education Research

How can young people learn about food sustainability from real farms, both on the farm and digitally? In my master's research I will make learning materials together with farmers and young people in Iceland, test them over one summer, and study what works. This is my draft research design from October 2026.

R1Working title and main question

Learning food sustainability on regenerative farms: co-designing farm-based and digital learning with farmers and young people in Iceland

Main question: How can learning materials co-designed by farmers and young people, both on the farm and digital, engage youth in food sustainability (local, organic and regenerative food) and open a path for those who want to farm?

  1. Landscape: what farm-based and food-sustainability education exists in Iceland and abroad, formal, non-formal and informal, and where are the gaps, especially for young people who want to enter farming?
  2. The visit: what do young people (both groups) and farmers value in hands-on farm learning, and how should materials be shaped around it?
  3. Digital: how do the partner farms communicate now, and which co-designed digital formats carry learning beyond the visit, and how do farmers see their value for the farm, including for selling their produce?

I will also ask, inside the interviews rather than as a separate question, what young people see as standing in their way if they want to farm: land, money, knowledge, or simply not knowing where to start. Their answers go into a short policy note and into later research.

R2Audiences

Audience 1

Learning food sustainability

Young people and the wider public who learn about sustainability, and food sustainability in particular, through real local, organic and regenerative farms.

Audience 2

Learning to farm

Young people who want the actual practices, including those who might farm professionally.

Secondary

Farmers

Co-designers of the materials, and learners about communicating what they do.

The “harbour”: young people in Iceland and the Nordic countries often have no idea where to go or where to start. In the long run I want one place that collects the useful links and shows clear routes into growing food and farming, starting from where you live: a balcony, a community garden like Seljagarður, an allotment, and then a real farm.

R3Theory chapters

  • T1
    Sustainability, narrowed to food sustainability
    Food systems, food security, SDG 2 and 12, Iceland's food policy to 2040. Organic is a certification, regenerative is a set of practices with no fixed standard, and local is about distance. The project teaches the difference.
    Literature
  • T2
    Education in all its forms
    Formal (school and upper-secondary curricula; adult and vocational education such as the Agricultural University of Iceland and Garðyrkjuskólinn á Reykjum), non-formal (Erasmus+, NGO projects, Climate Café Iceland), informal (libraries, community gardens, farm visits). Education for sustainability, place-based and experiential learning, youth participation as democratic practice.
    Literature
  • T3
    Existing programmes, Iceland first
    I look at what already exists first: how programmes run, what they offer and who pays for them. Iceland comes first, then the other Nordic countries, plus a few examples from the rest of Europe. I start with Inn på tunet (Norway), Nordic care farms and open-farm days, Matís's Krakkar kokka, LEAF Open Farm Sunday (UK), Estonia's Open Farm Day, InclusiGardens, FarmElder and Social Farming Ireland. I also include urban and community gardening, such as Seljagarður in Reykjavík.
    Literature
  • T4
    How farms communicate: social marketing and social business
    I say this openly: if content that teaches also helps a farm sell, that is exactly the win-win I am hoping for. Many farmers think of communication as money down the drain and only join in when there is a grant. If they can see the same content teaching and selling, they are more likely to put time into it. Social marketing (Kotler and Zaltman, 1971); small farms as social businesses.
    Literature
  • T5
    Digital learning formats
    Short video and microlearning, online learning paths, digital youth work; what small farms can keep up with little time.
    Literature
  • T6
    Barriers to entering farming
    Land, capital, subsidies and generational renewal in Iceland and Europe. This is background, and the basis for the policy note.
    Literature

R4Method: design-based research

How the three options fit together. I started with three possible studies and combined them. Option A (designing farm learning together with young people) is the core: it gives me the method and the main question. Option B (routes and barriers into farming) became sub-question 1, the review of what exists, plus some interview questions about barriers. Option C (how farms communicate online) became sub-question 3.

I will not collect sales figures. I explore the link between education and sales through the literature and through what farmers tell me. One summer can show whether the approach is promising; it cannot prove long-term effects. Interviews and surveys will only start after University of Iceland ethics approval.

R4bThe practical research, step by step

The fieldwork happens in three kinds of places: two partner farms (in South and North-East Iceland, still to be confirmed), a community growing plot near Reykjavík that we use as an open “playground” for trying things out, and a community garden in Reykjavík for the workshops.

Who takes part

Young people

About 15–25 people aged 16–30, in two groups: those curious about food sustainability, and those who want to grow food or farm. I will find them through youth centres, upper-secondary schools, universities and my own networks.

Who takes part

Farmers and growers

2–4 farmers or growers from the partner sites. They host and design with us, and they are paid for their time.

  1. Spring 2027
    1 · Baseline
    • Content audit of each farm's current channels: website, social media, newsletters. What do they say, how often, and to whom?
    • Short interview with each farmer: what they want people to learn, and how much time they realistically have.
  2. May – June
    2 · Co-design workshops (2 sessions)
    • Young people and farmers together. Map the questions young people have about food and farming; choose the topics.
    • Plan a farm visit and storyboard 3–5 short videos and a simple online learning path.
  3. June – August
    3 · Test (3–4 farm and garden days)
    • Each day: farm walk and the farm's story; a hands-on task (sowing, harvesting, composting, preserving); a short reflection circle.
    • Young people film and help edit the short videos; the learning path goes online for them to use after the visit.
  4. Aug – Sep
    4 · Evaluate
    • Interviews: about 10–12 young people from both groups, and all participating farmers. Includes what stops young people from going into farming.
    • A short questionnaire before and after: what participants know about local, organic and regenerative food, and whether they intend to grow, visit farms or buy locally.
    • Observation notes from each day, and digital engagement (video views, how many complete the learning path).

Analysis: I will use thematic analysis for the interviews and notes, and simple descriptive statistics for the questionnaires. In design-based research you revise the materials after each round, so what comes out is the materials themselves plus a set of design principles that have been tested in practice.

Ethics: University of Iceland approval, informed consent from everyone, parental consent for anyone under 18, separate consent for filming, and no names attached to quotes.

R7About the researcher

I'm Marina Ermina, a mixed-methods researcher and educator. My work sits where human wellbeing, sustainability and learning meet. I hold a first-class MA in Environment and Natural Resources from the University of Iceland (2025), and this study is the thesis for my second MA, in International Education.

In my earlier research I looked at how human wellbeing and ecological sustainability are measured in the Nordic countries and how the two connect. I compared wellbeing indices (HDI, SPI, SDG Index) with the Ecological Footprint and mapped national wellbeing-economy frameworks. I have also worked as a data analyst on the National Footprint and Biocapacity Accounts.

I co-founded Green Wellbeing, and through it I have run environmental and wellbeing workshops, including at Seljagarður community garden, SEEDS Iceland and GAIA Iceland. I have worked with children as a nature and wellbeing educator and done Erasmus+ training for youth trainers. I also grow food myself and work alongside regenerative farming in Iceland.
