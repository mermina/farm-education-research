Farm Education for Food Sustainability

How can the general public and young people who want to grow food or farm learn food sustainability from real farms, on site and digitally? The first stage of a co-design process with the general public and young people in Iceland.

★Why it matters

Why food. Food is one of our most basic human needs: without it there is no health, no learning and no life. The right to food is part of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Article 25 (United Nations, 1948), and ending hunger and making farming sustainable is one of the UN’s Sustainable Development Goals, SDG 2 (United Nations, n.d.). Almost all of our food starts on a farm. Sustainability means meeting the needs of today without taking away the ability of future generations to meet theirs (World Commission on Environment and Development [WCED], 1987), and food is the clearest example: if the way we farm wears out the soil, water and climate that farming depends on, we put our most basic need at risk.

The problem. And today, the way we produce food is one of the biggest pressures on the planet. Agrifood systems cause about one third of all greenhouse gas emissions caused by people: 32% in 2023 (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations [FAO], 2025). Agriculture also causes 80% of deforestation and uses 70% of the world’s fresh water, food systems are the leading cause of biodiversity loss, and up to 40% of the Earth’s land is already degraded (Carbon Brief, 2022). How we farm, and what we choose to eat, is part of the solution.

Iceland knows this problem well. Since settlement, woodland loss and grazing have led to soil erosion that is among the worst known outside the world’s drought regions (Arnalds et al., 2001). Food security is a concern too: Iceland grows about 43% of the vegetables it eats, and its farms depend on imported fuel and fertiliser (Stjórnarráð Íslands, 2021).

The government is moving. Iceland now has a Food Policy to 2040 (Alþingi, 2023a), an Agricultural Policy to 2040 that calls for direct links between producers and consumers (Alþingi, 2023b), and an organic action plan with a target of 10% organic farmland by 2040 (Matvælaráðuneytið, 2024). But none of their action plans includes learning for the general public, schools or farm visits.

Why everyone needs this, and who it is for. Food concerns all of us. Every day we choose what to buy, cook and throw away, and as citizens we help decide how food is produced. Research links food literacy (understanding where food comes from and how it is produced) with sustainability, including more sustainable diets (McManus et al., 2025), although knowledge alone is not enough: people also need real experience. Seeing a working farm helps. In Finland, a single farm visit significantly reduced urban pupils’ wrong ideas about farming (Smeds et al., 2015). So this learning is for everyone in Iceland, of all ages: to make informed everyday choices, and to take part in decisions about our food system.

It is also about who will farm next. In the EU, only about 12% of farm managers were under 40 in 2020 (EU CAP Network, 2024). In Iceland, research describes a continuous decline in the number of farmers and weakening rural communities (Karlsson, 2018). Interest in farming has to start somewhere, usually long before anyone is ready to commit to years of formal study at the Agricultural University or the horticulture school at Reykir. Most people are busy with jobs, studies and families, the information is scattered, and learning directly from farmers is rare. An easy first step, on a farm or with digital tools at your own pace, can grow that interest into a real choice.

What is missing. I mapped what exists in Iceland. No scheme pays farmers to host the public. The open-farm list has had no update since 2020 (Bændasamtök Íslands, n.d.). Matís’s farm-visit and video project Krakkar kokka ended in 2024 (Sveinsdóttir, Guðlaugsdóttir, et al., 2024). No platform brings Icelandic farmers’ knowledge together in one place for people to learn at their own pace. Other places, such as Bavaria in Germany (Staatliche Führungsakademie für Ernährung, Landwirtschaft und Forsten [FüAk], n.d.) and England in the UK (Rural Payments Agency & Natural England, 2025), pay farms for each educational visit.

What this study brings. A first step towards an easy way into sustainable food and farming: a co-designed map of learning opportunities for the general public and young people. It shapes two parts that work together: learning on the farm, through visits where people see, touch and ask about soil, compost, crop diversity and short supply chains; and a digital platform for learning at your own pace, with short videos from Icelandic farmers, clear explanations of organic, regenerative and agroecological farming, and links to take the next step. Both are designed together with farmers, the general public and young people, and both are meant to be useful to the farms themselves. As far as I could find, nothing in Iceland combines these parts.

The focus is the sustainable side of farming, organic or not, which I describe with FAO’s agroecology framework. Some farms that use good local compost cannot be certified, because Iceland’s organic action plan names the shortage of permitted fertilisers as the main obstacle (Matvælaráðuneytið, 2024). The study does not compare farming systems; it asks how this kind of learning can be designed.

R1Working title and main question

Learning food sustainability from Icelandic farms: the first stage of co-designing on-site and digital learning with farmers, the public and young people

Main question: How can learning opportunities on food sustainability (local, organic and regenerative food), on farms and digital, be co-designed with farmers and other growers, the general public and young people, so that they engage the public and open a path for young people who want to grow food or farm?

This master's covers the first stage of the co-design: what each group values and what farmers can offer, turned into a co-designed map of learning opportunities for the general public and young people. Developing the content in depth, with farmers and sustainability professionals, is a later, funded stage.

In this study, “farm” means any place where food is grown and people can learn there: commercial farms, and urban food-growing sites such as community gardens. Ornamental gardens are not included.

  1. What farm-based food-sustainability education exists in Iceland and abroad, formal, non-formal and informal, and where are the gaps, especially for young people who want to enter farming?
  2. What do the general public interested in food sustainability, young people who want to grow food or farm sustainably, and farmers and growers value in hands-on farm learning, and how should learning opportunities be shaped around it?
  3. How do the partner farms communicate now, and which co-designed digital formats carry learning beyond the visit, and how do farmers and growers see their value for the farm, including for selling their produce?

Built into the interviews, not a separate question: what young people see as barriers to entering farming (land, money, knowledge, not knowing where to start). This feeds a short policy note and future research.

R3Theory chapters

Read the full theory chapters with sources →

  • T1
    Sustainability, narrowed to food sustainability
    Sustainability first: what it means (Brundtland, 1987) and its environmental, social and economic sides. Then food systems and food security, SDG 2 and SDG 12, and Iceland's food policy to 2040. How farming affects the environment (soil, climate, biodiversity, water), and why local and sustainable food matters. Organic, ecological, regenerative and local have different definitions: organic is a certification (and “ecological” means certified organic in the Nordic languages), regenerative is a set of practices with no fixed standard, and local is about distance. They share one aim, sustainability. The project teaches both the differences and what they have in common.
    Literature
  • T2
    Education in all its forms
    Formal (school and upper-secondary curricula; adult and vocational education such as the Agricultural University of Iceland and Garðyrkjuskólinn á Reykjum), non-formal (Erasmus+, NGO projects, Climate Café Iceland), informal (libraries, community gardens, farm visits). Education for sustainability, place-based and experiential learning, youth participation as democratic practice.
    Literature
  • T3
    Existing programmes: Iceland and abroad
    Research what is available first: how they operate, what they offer, who funds them. Scope: Iceland first, then the other Nordic countries, with a few wider European examples. Start with Inn på tunet (Norway), Nordic care farms and open-farm days, Matís's Krakkar kokka, LEAF Open Farm Sunday (UK), Estonia's Open Farm Day, InclusiGardens, FarmElder and Social Farming Ireland. Include urban and community gardening programmes (e.g. Seljagarður in Reykjavík).
    Literature
  • T4
    How farms communicate: social marketing and social business
    Stated openly: if educational content also raises sales, that is the symbiosis we want. Many farmers see communication as wasted money and take part only with a grant; showing that the same content educates and sells makes them willing to invest. Social marketing (Kotler and Zaltman, 1971); small farms as social businesses.
    Literature
  • T5
    Digital learning formats
    Short video and microlearning, online learning paths, digital youth work; what small farms can keep up with little time.
    Literature
  • T6
    Barriers to entering farming
    Land access, capital, subsidies, generational renewal in Iceland and Europe. Context, and the basis for the policy note.
    Literature

R4Method: the first stage of a co-design process

Co-design in stages. This master's is stage 1 (Discover → Define in the Double Diamond; the “front end” of co-design in Sanders & Stappers, 2008): finding out what each group values and what farmers and growers can offer, and turning it into a co-designed map of learning opportunities for the general public and young people. Each group decides once, in sequence (sequential co-design in one round): the public and young people set the priorities, then the farms say what they can offer in answer, and the two are combined by a stated rule. Iteration comes in stage 2. Developing each opportunity in depth with farmers and sustainability professionals, and testing it, is stage 2 and needs funding.

R4bThe practical research, step by step

Where it happens: three contrasting food-growing sites, studied as cases: Seljagarður (a neighbourhood garden and greenhouse in Seljahverfi, Reykjavík, run by volunteers and residents since 2014, with outdoor and greenhouse plots for rent), Reykjalundur (an ecological, no-till vegetable farm in Grímsnes, South Iceland, selling through a weekly vegetable subscription), and Syðra-Holt (Yrkja, a certified organic and biodynamic vegetable farm in Svarfaðardalur, North Iceland, also selling vegetable boxes and sauerkraut). More farms are welcome as the project opens doors.

Who takes part

Two groups

One survey and the live sessions are open to everyone; a screening question at the start sorts each answer into a group: (1) members of the general public interested in food sustainability, and (2) young people aged 16–30 who want to grow food or farm sustainably. Aim: 50–150 answers in all, with at least 20 in group 2. People are reached where they already meet: universities, the young farmers' association, libraries, community gardens, markets and youth venues.

Who takes part

Farmers and growers

One main contact at each farm (two at most), and 2–4 people from Seljagarður, as co-designers and hosts, paid for their time. Short interviews with other farmers where possible.

Goal: to find out what each site can realistically offer, in answer to what the public and young people asked for. How: a semi-structured interview of about 45 minutes on the farm, and, if the farmer agrees, a short farm walk with observation notes on how the work is done. Topics: what they want people to learn; which of the audiences’ choices they could offer (visits, hands-on days, videos); how much time and help they have; how they communicate now; and whether learning could also help the farm, for example in selling produce. What they need: to be asked at a quiet time, to be paid for their time, and clear limits on what they agree to.

  1. Mar – May 2027
    1 · Survey
    • A screening question sorts people into the two groups. Questions on interests, formats, barriers, and where people want to learn: in town, on a farm, both or digitally.
    • Demand questions for partner sites, such as whether people would join and pay for a community-garden course.
  2. Mar – May
    2 · Live sessions (2–4)
    • Held inside events and classes that already run. A farmer talks about the work, then anonymous live polling, slide by slide: ranking topics, choosing formats, deciding what comes first.
    • An open dialogue with the farmer, recorded as written notes, and the results shown back to the room.
  3. June – July 2027
    3 · Interviews at the three sites (summer, on the farm)
    • About 45 minutes each. Each farmer sees a one-page summary of what the public and young people chose, and is asked: which of these could you offer, and how? Also what they want people to learn, and how much time they realistically have.
    • A look at each site's current channels (website, social media, newsletters): what they say, how often, and to whom.
  4. July
    4 · Shared map of learning opportunities
    • Each group decides once, in sequence: the audiences set the priorities, the farms set what is feasible. The two are combined by a stated rule: wanted and offered goes onto the map; wanted but not offered is a gap for stage 2; offered but not wanted is optional. Nothing waits on a second round.
    • If live sessions are hard to arrange, the online ranking round carries the decisions instead.

Analysis: descriptive statistics for the survey and polling, compared across the groups; thematic analysis of interviews and session notes, reported per site. The sites are a multiple case study chosen for contrast (urban community garden, an uncertified regenerative farm in the south, a certified organic and biodynamic farm in the north), and the sample size is justified by information power (Malterud, Siersma & Guassora, 2016).

Ethics: University of Iceland ethics approval before any data collection; anonymous answers.

R5Outcome

Output

Co-designed map of learning opportunities for the general public and young people

Not a fixed course: a map of what they want to learn about food growing and food sustainability, and what farms and growers can offer. It could include, for example, making kimchi, learning about herbs, farm visits, hands-on farm days or short videos, depending on what people ask for. Turning it into courses and materials is a later stage that needs resources and grant funding. This map shapes the harbour (below).

Output

Policy note and letters to authorities

Barriers to entering farming (land, money, subsidies), and the support farmers need to offer learning on their farms, such as paid time for hosting and regular grants. Sent to government and the relevant authorities.

The harbour (digital platform): the researcher's own initiative

Many people in Iceland and the Nordic countries, young people especially, are not sure where to go or where to start. As a result of this research, the researcher is building the harbour as her own initiative, alongside and after the thesis: one place that brings together the learning materials and useful links, with paths into farming. The focus is real farms with a sustainable focus. Most people live in towns, so the path can start where they are, in a food-growing place such as Seljagarður or on the platform itself, and lead on to real farms.

It is also meant as a place for farmers: information on grants and support published regularly, and a shared voice for the support farmers need to offer learning on their farms, linked to the policy note.

The thesis does not promise the harbour. It provides the evidence and the co-designed map of learning opportunities for the general public and young people that shape it, and the harbour is where the findings are put to use: Iceland first, later the Nordic countries.

R7About the researcher

Marina Ermina is a mixed-methods researcher and educator working where human wellbeing, sustainability and learning meet. She holds a first-class MA in Environment and Natural Resources from the University of Iceland (2025) and is completing a second MA in International Education, of which this study is the thesis.

Her earlier research asked how human wellbeing and ecological sustainability are measured and connected in the Nordic countries, comparing wellbeing indices (HDI, SPI, SDG Index) with the Ecological Footprint and mapping national wellbeing-economy frameworks. She has also worked as a data analyst on the National Footprint and Biocapacity Accounts.

She has run environmental and wellbeing workshops (including at Seljagarður community garden, SEEDS Iceland and GAIA Iceland), worked as a nature and wellbeing educator with children, and completed Erasmus+ training for youth trainers. She grows food herself and works alongside regenerative farming in Iceland.
